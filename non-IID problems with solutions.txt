1. TEXT (TABULAR)
1. Feature-space heterogeneity 
PB: Sites have different feature sets
EG: One hospital records “Blood Pressure” and “Heart Rate,” another records “Glucose” and “BMI.”
SL: - fixed 8 features + 8 corresponding mask features (for existing or not)
    - for existing features: z-scalling with global mean & var, then mask as 1
    - for missing features: fill with 0, then mask as 0

2. Label-space heterogeneity
PB: Sites have different label sets or class coverage
EG: One site has labels {0,1}, another has {2,3}. 
SL: - local head design.

3. Statistical heterogeneity (non-IID)
PB: Sites share the same feature schema but data follow different distributions	
EG: All hospitals record the same 20 columns, but one site’s patients are mostly older and diabetic, while another’s are younger and healthy.
    Different sites decleares different importance of the 8 features.
SL: - 1. the 8 mask features
    - They tell the encoder which parts of the input it can trust and which parts are just placeholders.
    - 2. global aggregation

4. Quantity heterogeneity
PB: Sites have different numbers of samples, leads to imblance site importance
EG: One hospital has 5,000 rows, another 500.
SL: - solved by aggregation

# z-scalling privacy issue:
- Z-scaling itself doesn’t leak privacy,
- but you must make sure the global mean and variance are calculated in a federated-safe way —
  sites share only simple sums or encrypted values, not their raw data.

- With this setup, everyone uses consistent normalization, and no one exposes private patient info.

0. object / organ itself: no non-IID problem.

1. Device / camera difference	Different hospitals use different fundus cameras, lenses, or lighting setups.	Site A’s images are bright and sharp, Site B’s are darker or have a red tone.
Resolution / preprocessing difference	Some datasets are cropped, some resized, some blurred.	One site keeps the whole eye, another zooms into the macula region.
Population difference	Patients’ skin tones, age ranges, or disease stages differ.	Site A: mostly older diabetics; Site B: younger patients with early symptoms.
Label distribution difference	Each site may have more or fewer “disease-positive” cases.	One site has 80 % normal, another has 60 % severe DR.
Noise & annotation quality	Labeling standards or doctors differ.	A mild case may be labeled “moderate” in one site but “mild” in another.


these influences are potential risks, not guaranteed problems.
Things like:
stretching the retina shape,
color tone differences,
lighting variation,
sample imbalance,
are not automatic errors — they’re just sources of uncertainty that might cause trouble depending on the task and the data sensitivity.
If your model’s job is simple — for example:
“Classify healthy vs. diseased retina,”

then small visual differences (a bit more round or oval) usually don’t matter much,
because CNNs are robust and can still find disease spots or blood vessel patterns correctly.

But if your task is more sensitive — say:
“Measure vessel diameter” or “locate optic disc shape,”
then distortion starts to matter, because those pixel-level shapes carry meaning.
